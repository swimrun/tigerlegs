About TigerLegs

What it does

TigerLegs splits a swimrun course into its swim and run legs. Upload a GPX file and you get the course on a map and a table with the distance of every swim (in metres) and every run (in kilometres), with totals at the bottom.

TIGERLegs stands for Terrain-Informed Gpx Extractor: Lets Go Swimrun. A tiger is amphibious and a good swimmer, and the name nods to Team Groot Rondje Eilanden.

How to use it

  1. Choose a .gpx file, or try the Rhederlaag sample track.
  2. Read the segments in the table. Hover over a row to highlight it on the map.
  3. Switch Include breaks on to see pauses as separate rows. Off by default.

How it finds swims and runs

TigerLegs combines several strategies and shows which one found each segment in the Detected by column:

  • Waypoints: course makers sometimes mark swim entries and exits in the file.
  • Speed: swimming is usually slower than running, but the difference is small, so speed alone is often not enough.
  • Landscape: OpenStreetMap water areas show where the track is actually in water. This is what makes it terrain-informed.

Segments of 0 m are dropped, and two swims in a row are merged into one.

Limits you should know about

  • The result is an estimate. Check it against the official course description.
  • Tracks with few points (sparse tracks) can hide short swims or runs, because points far apart do not show what happened in between.
  • Only lakes and rivers drawn as areas in OpenStreetMap are recognised. Sea and coast are not handled yet.
  • If the water lookup is unavailable, TigerLegs falls back to speed and waypoints only.

Your data

Your GPX file is analysed in your browser and is not uploaded. To find water, TigerLegs sends only the rough area of the track (a bounding box, about 300 m wider than the track) to the Overpass API, which serves OpenStreetMap data. Overpass can see your IP address, like any website. Your device location is never read.
